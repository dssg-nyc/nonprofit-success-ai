DSSG NYC · AI Lifecycle Platform

Nonprofit Success AI

System Design Document

A CSM-grade lifecycle platform for every pro bono engagement DSSG NYC runs — a client portal backed by three focused AI agents, a shared skill/tool runtime, centralized evaluation, and a small set of shared deterministic services. The design deliberately separates reasoning from execution: AI recommends, summarizes, classifies, and drafts; application services own state, permissions, scheduling, contracts, tasks, and external side effects. This revision removes overlapping agent responsibilities, eliminates duplicate capability pipelines, and aligns the application to a shared Supabase identity/database foundation with GrantPilot before the shared-user pilot expands.

01 — Why This Exists

Every nonprofit enters through the same bottleneck

Today, intake runs on whichever volunteer has an hour free — not on a process. Nonprofits arrive with every level of data maturity, problem clarity, and capacity, and whoever picks up the email decides the bucket and the fit from scratch, every time. Nothing is captured or reused, so each new volunteer re-learns the same judgment calls — and the organizations with the least data maturity, who often need DSSG most, quietly fall through the cracks.

The fix: route, onboard, and support nonprofit partners with the same rigor as a CSM playbook — built as five AI agents spanning Qualify › Onboard › Adopt › Expand › Renew › Advocate.

Time back to volunteers

Triage and onboarding-prep move from a recurring, ad hoc task to a system that runs itself — freeing volunteer hours for the actual project work.

Better matches, faster starts

Every nonprofit gets a consistent readiness check and a bucket that fits, instead of whatever the on-call volunteer guesses that week.

Social impact through inclusion

Advisory is a real first-tier offering, not a rejection path — the earliest-stage nonprofits are never turned away.

Under $50/month to run. And human-in-the-loop by design — every agent drafts and recommends; a person approves anything that reaches a partner.

02 — The Lifecycle

One journey, seven controlled gates

What a nonprofit experiences as a single journey is, underneath, two connected systems: a public, pre-account triage step, and a six-stage roadmap inside the portal once they're a client. The lifecycle remains the product spine. AI agents attach to decision points; deterministic services own execution and state transitions.

Public · pre-account · no login

In portal · authenticated · engagements/{businessId}_{stage}

Stage 0 — Public Intake

ScoutHITL L2 / L3

A prospective organization fills out a 10-question form at /apply — five to seven minutes, no account required. Scout scores the response into one of five buckets plus an independent readiness signal, then writes a single scoutIntakes/{id} document holding the raw answers, its own routing output, and (later) a reviewer's decision.

Where Scout sits: entirely before the funnel — there's no logged-in user, and no Business or Engagement record exists yet. An admin works the queue at /scout/review and approves, edits, or rejects-and-redirects. Converting an approved intake into an onboarded client is still a manual step today — there's no auto-created Business record on approval yet.

Stage 1 — Initial Meeting

ArchitectHITL L3

The kickoff call. This is where Architect's 18-question Current State Assessment gets conducted live by staff (~30 minutes) — distinct from Scout's 5–7 minute public triage. The assessment scores five maturity dimensions and generates a charter plus a 90-day plan, written to architectAssessments/{id}, where the ID matches the source scoutIntakes document.

New in this revision: once staff approve the charter, it's populated into an Engagement Contract for the nonprofit's authorized rep to preview and sign — see Section 04. Stage 1 doesn't close, and Stage 2 doesn't unlock, until that signature is on file.

Stage 2 — Budget Check

Health Service

Tracked as an engagements/{businessId}_budget_check document with a status (pending / in_progress / completed), free-text notes, and a budget_amount field. No agent owns this stage yet — the shared Health Service can flag a budget conversation that's stalled.

Stage 3 — Data Ethics Committee

Health Service

Same document pattern as Budget Check, with stage: 'data_ethics_committee'. Worth flagging for the room: this often gets described conversationally as one combined "budget/ethics check," but the roadmap treats them as two separate, sequential gates.

Stage 4 — Scoping

ArchitectProject Scoping

Where Architect's generated 90-day plan gets operationalized — build-basics, ship-one-deliverable, remediation-only, or accelerate, depending on the maturity score. Problem filtering and impact ranking (formerly pitched as its own Sprint 4) is simply what Architect is doing here — one capability, not a separate hand-off.

Stage 5 — Hackathon Ready

Communications / Ops ServicesResource PlanningMarketing Automation

The hackathon_project field activates. Venue/food/tech logistics and Eventbrite-plus-social promotion — formerly Sprints 5 and 6 — are handled by the shared Operations and Communications Services, still gated at L4 (human decides, agent assists), since all of it is partner-visible.

Stage 6 — Membership

ChronicleHITL L3

Chronicle's impact wrap-up: memos, case studies, and lessons that feed back into Scout's onboarding kits — turning this single engagement into institutional memory for the next one. This is the mechanism that closes the loop the problem statement opened with.

03 — The Unified Framework

Three AI agents, shared services, zero duplicate ownership

The original five-agent model is over-decomposed for the current scale. the former Pulse and Envoy capabilities are useful, but they do not need independent reasoning loops: health monitoring is a deterministic analytics service with optional AI interpretation, while communications, calendar, logistics, and marketing are tool-driven execution services. Chronicle remains an AI agent because impact synthesis and institutional learning require cross-engagement reasoning.

S

Scout

Qualification & routingBuildingHITL L2/L3Intake Classification›

Owns: public intake interpretation, service-bucket classification, readiness assessment, confidence scoring, and recommended routing. Scout has exactly one decision boundary: Should DSSG accept this intake, and what should happen next?

Does not own: scheduling, portfolio task management, meeting transcription, communications, or engagement health. Those are shared services triggered after a routing decision.

Output: a versioned IntakeDecision containing bucket, readiness, confidence, rationale, flags, and recommended next action. The model runs server-side; the browser never writes an AI-generated decision directly.

A

Architect

Assessment & delivery planningBuildingHITL L3Meeting IntelligenceProject Scoping›

Owns: Current State Assessment interpretation, maturity scoring, charter generation, 90-day plan generation, and scope recommendations. Meeting transcripts/notes are inputs to Architect rather than a separate agent capability.

Action Management is a service, not an agent: Architect creates structured tasks; the shared task service stores owners, dates, status, and audit history. Health signals read that same record instead of maintaining a second task system.

Output: versioned Assessment, Charter, and Plan artifacts. Human approval is required before a charter or plan becomes operative.

C

Chronicle

Engagement intelligence & institutional learningProposedHITL L3Impact SynthesisKnowledge Retrieval›

Owns: end-of-engagement synthesis, impact memos, case-study drafts, lessons learned, and retrieval of prior comparable engagements when planning a new one.

Does not own: routine monitoring or outbound communications. A scheduled health service computes objective signals; a communications service drafts/sends messages only after explicit workflow approval.

Output: versioned ImpactMemo and structured Lesson records. Only approved lessons enter the reusable knowledge base.

04 — Shared Agent Runtime

One shared bucket of skills, plugins, MCPs, and policies

The agents are not separate software ecosystems. Scout, Architect, and Chronicle execute inside one governed runtime and consume the same versioned catalog of skills, tools, plugins, MCP servers, prompts, policies, and evaluation suites. This prevents duplicated tool wrappers, inconsistent permissions, and agent-specific implementations of the same capability.

Shared assetSingle source of truthRule
SkillsVersioned skills repository / registryReusable capability packages with input/output schemas, instructions, permissions, tests, owner, and version
PluginsCentral plugin registryOne connector per external system; agents request capabilities rather than implementing their own integrations
MCP serversCentral MCP catalog + gatewayShared discovery, authentication, rate limits, scopes, logging, and allowlists
Prompts / policiesVersioned prompt and policy registryPrompts are deployable artifacts with owners, versions, rollback, and evaluation coverage
ModelsCentral model gatewayAgents select from approved models through one interface; no hard-coded vendor SDKs inside skills
Memory / knowledgeShared knowledge serviceOnly approved, provenance-tagged artifacts become reusable organizational memory
ApprovalsCentral policy engine + review queueRisk level is determined by action type, not by which agent happens to invoke it

Key design principle: agents are consumers of shared capabilities, not owners of infrastructure. If Calendar, Gmail, Slack, Eventbrite, GitHub, or a future MCP tool is needed by two agents, it is implemented once and exposed through the shared registry.

05 — Quality, Evaluation & Safety

Evaluation is a deployment gate, not a dashboard

Every agent and shared skill must have a measurable quality contract. The system should use deterministic tests first, model-based judges second, and human review for consequential behavior. No prompt, model, skill, or tool change reaches production solely because a demo looks good.

LevelCheckExamplesRelease rule
Q0Schema / unit testsJSON schema validity, required fields, permissions, deterministic scoring, tool argument validationMust pass 100%
Q1Golden-set regressionCurated Scout intakes, CSA assessments, charter examples, impact summariesNo material regression from approved baseline
Q2LLM-as-a-judgeCorrectness, groundedness, completeness, instruction adherence, tone, rubric complianceMinimum score threshold + no critical failures
Q3Safety / adversarial testsPrompt injection, malicious tool requests, unauthorized data access, fabricated citations, PII leakageZero critical policy violations
Q4Human acceptancePartner-facing drafts, legal/contract artifacts, consequential recommendationsExplicit human approval before release
Q5Production monitoringLatency, failure rate, tool errors, override rate, approval rate, judge drift, costAutomatic alert / rollback thresholds

Judge design

Use a dedicated evaluator model or ensemble for rubric-based checks, but never treat an LLM judge as ground truth. Judge outputs should be calibrated against human-labeled examples, versioned alongside the prompt/model under test, and supplemented by deterministic assertions. For critical workflows, require both judge pass + human approval.

Offline eval

Run on every skill/prompt/model change against fixed golden datasets before deployment.

Shadow eval

Run a candidate model in parallel without affecting production decisions to compare quality and cost.

Online eval

Track real outcomes, staff overrides, partner corrections, and downstream completion rates after launch.

06 — Consent & Legal

Turning the charter into a signed commitment

Once staff approve Architect's charter and 90-day plan at the end of Stage 1, the nonprofit's authorized representative needs to formally accept it before the roadmap advances. That's a different kind of gate from anything else in this document — not staff overseeing an agent, but the client's own legal consent. The actual contract language is still to come from DSSG NYC; what's specified here is the mechanism it runs through.

Recommendation: preview and sign in-app, not download-and-email

Option A

Download, sign, email back

  • Zero build cost — works today with a PDF and a mailto link
  • Feels familiar to EDs used to wet-ink workflows
  • Relies on someone remembering to send it, and staff manually matching the reply back to the right engagement
  • No automatic stage-gating — someone has to notice the email arrived and flip the status by hand
Recommended Option B

Preview & sign in-app

  • No context-switch — reviewed and executed in the same session, right where the charter already lives
  • Server timestamp + typed name become part of an immutable record automatically, same audit-trail pattern already used for architectAssessments
  • The signature event itself can gate the Stage 1 → Stage 2 transition — no one has to remember to update anything
  • Directly serves the deck's own stated goal: time back to volunteers

Best of both: keep a "Download PDF" button on the in-app version for the signer's own records, and have the system auto-email the fully executed document to the signer and to dssgnyc@gmail.com the moment it's signed. That gets the email paper trail without making the round-trip the primary mechanism. A simple typed-name-plus-checkbox e-signature with a server timestamp is generally sufficient under the U.S. ESIGN Act and UETA for an agreement like this — worth a quick confirmation from DSSG's counsel once the real contract text exists, since I'm not a lawyer and can't rule on the specific document.

07 — Oversight

Human oversight is the design constraint, not an add-on

Every agent action maps to one of four levels. Escalation increases with partner-facing stakes — nothing reaches a nonprofit partner without the appropriate level of human judgment in the loop. This is staff overseeing an agent; it's a different axis from Section 04's contract signature, which is the client's own consent, not a staff review.

L1

Agent acts, human notified

Internal health signals, telemetry, non-partner-facing housekeeping
L2

Agent acts, reversible

Scout high-confidence classification and other low-risk internal transitions
L3

Agent drafts, human approves

Architect plans · Chronicle impact memos · low-confidence recommendations
L4

Human decides, agent assists

Partner-facing communications, commitments, publishing, contracts, and consequential decisions

08 — Application Architecture

One application, one source of truth, thin AI layer

The most important implementation change is to stop treating the AI framework as the architecture. The application owns workflow state and permissions; AI is a server-side capability invoked at specific decision points.

LayerRecommended implementationDecision
Web appReact + TypeScript + existing routing/UIKeep
IdentitySupabase AuthAdopt now as the shared identity provider with GrantPilot
System of recordSupabase PostgresAdopt now as the canonical shared application database
Server boundaryCloud Run/Edge Functions/server APIRequired for model calls, privileged writes, integrations, and agent execution
Workflow state machineTyped application workflow + Cloud Tasks for delayed workPrefer over multi-agent orchestration
AI gatewayOne internal model adapter with structured-output validationDo not introduce LangChain + LlamaIndex + LiteLLM simultaneously
IntegrationsSmall TypeScript adapters for Calendar, email, Eventbrite/social as actually neededAdd only when a workflow requires them
KnowledgeSupabase Postgres + pgvector when needed; object/document storage for source artifactsStart simple; enable retrieval incrementally
ObservabilityCentral event log + model request/response metadata + approval audit trailRequired from MVP

What should be removed from the target stack

  • Firebase + Supabase as parallel sources of truth: avoid. Because GrantPilot already uses Supabase and the two apps must share identity, migrate Success Portal AI to the same Supabase project rather than maintaining two auth/database systems.
  • CrewAI: unnecessary for three bounded agents that are invoked at explicit workflow steps; it adds orchestration complexity without solving a current problem.
  • LangChain + LlamaIndex + LiteLLM as a bundle: use one thin model adapter first. Add a framework only when a demonstrated requirement justifies it.
  • Looker Studio + Retool for the core product: keep the operational review UI inside the React application; use BI tools later for reporting.
  • Multiple meeting vendors: select one capture source for the pilot and normalize its output into a single transcript schema.

Target flow: React → Supabase Auth session → server-side agent/runtime endpoint → workflow/state transition → skill/tool retrieval → one or more bounded AI calls → validated structured result → Supabase/Postgres transaction → approval queue or deterministic side effect. No agent-to-agent free-form conversations.

09 — Data & Security

Six collections, default-deny rules

Supabase/Postgres uses RLS, database constraints, server-side functions, and a single public intake boundary. Agent-generated decisions are never trusted from the browser.

Business

One registered org / small business
  • id, name string
  • type 'small_business' | 'nonprofit'
  • ein, industry, address optional
  • ownerId immutable after create
  • certified boolean
  • createdAt timestamp

Engagement

One roadmap-stage record per business
  • id, businessId, ownerId string
  • stage one of the six roadmap stages
  • status pending | in_progress | completed
  • notes, budget_amount, hackathon_project optional
  • updatedAt timestamp

ScoutIntake

Raw answers + Scout's output + review decision, one doc
  • 10 intake answers org, mission, need…
  • bucket, confidence, rationale Scout output
  • poc_score, clarity_score, foothold_score
  • composite_signal, flags, hitlTier
  • review decision added by staff

ArchitectAssessment

Doc ID matches its source ScoutIntake ID
  • Scout handoff bucket, confidence, readiness
  • 18 CSA answers
  • 5 maturity scores + composite band
  • generated charter
  • generated 90-day plan

EngagementContract new

Doc ID matches its Engagement ID · immutable once signed
  • engagementId, businessId string
  • charterVersion ref to source charter
  • signerName, signerTitle string
  • signedAt server timestamp, not client
  • status unsigned | signed
  • pdfUrl generated on sign

Security rules, by collection

  • users/{userId} — read/write own profile only; role immutable after creation.
  • businesses/{businessId} — owner-scoped; ownerId must match the creator and can't change afterward.
  • engagements/{engagementId} — owner-scoped; creation requires the referenced business to already exist; a completed engagement can't revert to an earlier status.
  • scoutIntakes/{intakeId} — the one public-write collection: anyone can create; only staff can read, list, or update, and updates are limited to review-decision fields.
  • architectAssessments/{id} — fully admin-only; creation requires a matching ScoutIntake doc to exist; no delete, for an audit trail.
  • engagementContracts/{id} — owner or admin can create and read; signedAt is set server-side only, never trusted from the client; once status == 'signed' the document rejects all further writes, including from admins.
Known gap: Scout's routing result must be generated server-side and persisted only after schema validation. The public intake endpoint may create raw intake data, but it must never accept a client-supplied AI classification, confidence, or approval state as authoritative.

08 — Org Governance

A platform boundary, not another AI operating system

Nonprofit Success AI should plug into JARVIS OS as a department application and skill catalog, but it should not reproduce JARVIS's orchestration, memory, or governance layers locally.

Nonprofit Success AIDepartment applicationBuild here
Owns nonprofit lifecycle state, portal UI, domain workflows, agent prompts, approval rules, and partner-facing records.
AI Decision LayerScout · Architect · ChronicleThree agents
Bounded reasoning components with typed inputs/outputs. No autonomous agent-to-agent delegation.
Shared ServicesTasks · Health · Communications · IntegrationsBuild once
Deterministic services own state and side effects. These are reusable platform primitives, not agents.
JARVIS OSCross-department governanceInterface only
Provides shared governance conventions, executive reporting, and future cross-department skills. It should not require this app to migrate its database or duplicate its workflow engine.

11 — Knowledge & Memory

Start with retrieval, not a knowledge graph

The proposed vector index and knowledge graph are useful long-term, but they are not prerequisites for the MVP. The first knowledge problem is simpler: retrieve approved prior engagements, templates, and lessons with clear provenance.

Phase 1

Keep canonical engagement records in Supabase Postgres (Firebase migration complete). Add a normalized knowledge document for approved templates, case studies, lessons, and decision rules. Retrieve only from approved content and store source IDs with every AI-generated recommendation.

Phase 2

Add the knowledge layer: pgvector migration, hybrid BM25 + vector retrieval with RRF, Granola meeting-note ingestion via webhook, and a read-only MCP surface for volunteers. Measure recall@10 on a golden set before widening rollout. Add graph relationships only for questions that genuinely require multi-hop relationships; do not build a graph because the architecture diagram has a box for one.

Critical governance rule: raw partner data, transcripts, and AI outputs are not automatically promoted into reusable organizational knowledge. Only approved, provenance-linked artifacts can influence future recommendations.

11 — Shared Identity & Backend Strategy

Move to Supabase now, but use one shared project

Because GrantPilot already uses Supabase and Success Portal AI is expected to share the same login and user base, the cleanest long-term architecture is one Supabase Auth tenant and one canonical Postgres backend. Do not build a Firebase-to-Supabase bridge as the permanent design.

Recommended

Put GrantPilot and Success Portal AI on the same Supabase project/Auth instance. Use auth.users as the identity root and separate application tables for profiles, organizations, memberships, engagements, grants, tasks, approvals, and agent artifacts. Enforce tenant and role boundaries with Postgres RLS.

Transition

Migrate Success Portal's Firebase Auth users and Firestore data in a controlled cutover. Supabase documents a Firebase Auth migration path, but test password/OAuth identity mapping, user IDs, sessions, and permissions before switching production traffic.

Important: shared userbase does not mean every application gets unrestricted access to every table. Each app gets its own service/API surface, and RLS plus server-side authorization enforces organization, role, and record-level boundaries.

Migration sequence

  1. Create the canonical Supabase schema and RLS policies first.
  2. Confirm GrantPilot and Success Portal can resolve the same auth.users.id for a test cohort.
  3. Migrate Firebase users and map OAuth identities; preserve an explicit legacy-ID mapping during transition.
  4. Backfill Firestore records into Postgres with deterministic IDs and provenance fields.
  5. Run both systems in read-only/shadow mode and reconcile counts, permissions, and critical workflows.
  6. Switch Success Portal writes to Supabase, monitor, then retire Firebase writes.

10 — Recommended Build Plan

Prove the workflow before expanding the AI

Phase 0 — Hardening

Move Scout inference server-side; enforce server-side authorization; define the canonical schemas; create the centralized approval queue; add immutable audit events. This is the immediate blocker to a real pilot.

Phase 1 — Pilot

Ship Scout end-to-end plus Architect assessment/plan generation. Automate approved-intake → Business → Engagement creation. Keep scheduling, communications, contracts, and tasks deterministic.

Phase 2 — Operational automation

Add the shared Task and Health services, one calendar integration, one communications channel, and the contract gate. Introduce Chronicle only after the core engagement records are reliable.

Phase 3 — Learning

Add the knowledge layer: pgvector migration, hybrid BM25 + vector retrieval with RRF, Granola meeting-note ingestion, and a read-only MCP surface for volunteers in Claude Code. Measure recall@10 on a golden set before widening rollout. Only then consider a knowledge graph, additional integrations, or a generalized orchestration platform.

Feasibility verdict

  • Core concept: High feasibility. Intake routing, structured assessment, plan generation, approvals, and lifecycle tracking are straightforward bounded AI/application workflows.
  • Original five-agent target: Medium feasibility. The functions are achievable, but five independent agents plus multiple frameworks create more operational complexity than the nonprofit workflow warrants.
  • Full target stack in 3–5 weeks: Low feasibility. The document itself notes unresolved hosting, review UI, two repositories/stacks, server-side Scout, missing knowledge infrastructure, and pending legal text. fileciteturn2file3L163-L187
  • Recommended MVP in 3–5 weeks: Feasible. Limit the release to Scout + Architect, one backend, one AI adapter, one approval surface, and the automated hand-off. The rest should be interfaces and backlog items.

Non-negotiable implementation rules

  1. AI never writes directly to partner-visible state without a server-side authorization and approval check.
  2. Every capability has exactly one system of record and one owning service.
  3. Agents return structured, schema-validated outputs; no free-form agent-to-agent handoffs.
  4. All side effects are idempotent and auditable.
  5. Every generated artifact stores model/version metadata, source references, reviewer, and approval status.
  6. Do not add infrastructure until a measured workflow requires it.

Bottom line: keep the product vision, simplify the machinery. The strongest implementation is not five autonomous agents; it is a reliable lifecycle application with three bounded reasoning components sitting on top of a single source of truth and a small set of deterministic services.
